As we all know,
an attention block in a neural network lets us
combine one word with other related words.
For example, in "I love machine learning",
the word "learning" can pay attention to "machine",
and use that information to understand what kind of learning we mean.
That is quite simple and powerful.

And this pattern has been around for nearly 10 years.
So the question today is: can we accelerate it?
With some kind of mathematical trick?

The answer is Yes.

---

So before that, let's think again: what exactly is an attention block?
In my opinion, we can just think of this complex mathematical structure as a simple idea: information transmission.
The job of an attention block is to pass information from one word to the other words that can attend to it.
And then, for the next word, we do it again.

---

So, based on that assumption, can we just move the information
with fewer computations?
A full attention block needs n^2 pairwise comparisons.
Even causal attention still needs roughly (n^2)/2.
The computational complexity is still quadratic.

So many of these connections are computed over and over again.
But my human instinct says: when I read words or sentences, I rarely go back over every word.
For example, when I read "I love machine learning",
I read "I love", then "machine learning", and put the two parts together.
I don't go back and compare "I" with "machine", then "I" with "learning", then every other pair.
So a bold idea was born.
Maybe we don't need all those dense connections.
Maybe we just need to make sure all the information gets connected at least once.

---

So I went and looked into it.
And yes, all kinds of "sparse attention" already exist.
Other people's work was already pretty close to what I had in mind.
And their results support the idea: a lot of those dense connections are redundant.

For example, the Sparse Transformer paper found that a full-attention model had already learned sparse patterns in many layers.
Their fixed sparse version even scored slightly better: 0.99 bits per byte versus 1.00 for dense attention.

And PowerAttention used about 94 percent sparsity, while perplexity only went from 9.42 to 10.00.

And other studies like these support the same idea.

So the idea that a lot of that computation is redundant is already widely accepted.

But after looking around, I didn't find quite the combination I was looking for:
spread information quickly, avoid spreading the same information over and over,
and keep the rules regular enough to turn into efficient GPU kernels.

And that happens to give you a reason to watch this video.

If I were just repeating what's already out there, you could just ask AI.
No need to watch my video.

So, here's my attempt to fill that gap in sparse attention.

I'll call it Staged Binary-Local Diffusion Attention.
A bit of a mouthful. But the idea is simple.

---

Here's how it works.
Instead of connecting every word to every earlier word, I give each layer a simple jumping rule.

In the first layer, each word looks at itself and one position to the left.
In the second layer, it looks at itself and two positions to the left.
Then the long jump doubles again: four, eight, sixteen, and so on.

But wait. If I only jump one, two, four, eight... what about three?
Did I just forget a word?

No. Three is one plus two.
The information can travel one position in the first layer, then another two in the second.
Seven? One plus two plus four.
Every distance can be built from these binary jumps.

And the word at the other end isn't carrying just its own information anymore.
It's also carrying information it picked up in earlier layers.
So I don't need to ask every word directly. I can let them pass the information along.

That's the binary part.
But passing information farther isn't enough. I also want to keep nearby words working together.
Now here's the staged, local part.

The first two layers use just those two sources.
From layer three onward, I also keep the two nearest words on the left.
So each word looks at four sources: itself, its two left neighbors, and one long-distance source.

The nearby connections stay nearby. The long jump keeps doubling.
I keep a small local route to organize the words, while the binary route spreads information farther and farther.

And I'm still using attention to choose how much information to take from each source.
The connections are fixed. Their weights are learned.

So I'm not replacing attention with a plain average.
I'm giving it a much smaller, more organized set of choices.

With the binary scaffold alone, the reachable range can double with each layer.
Two positions, four, eight, sixteen...
Ten layers can cover 1024 positions. Twenty can cover about a million.
All without asking each position to directly compare itself with a million others.

And the rule is the same at every position in a layer.
No searching for neighbors. No sorting words into groups.
Just fixed offsets, which I can calculate directly.


That's the idea: a few regular connections, a rapidly expanding reach, and a small local route to keep the information organized.
The local route keeps nearby information organized. The long-distance route keeps expanding.

In short, it's a diffusion step along the time axis.

---

So, in theory, the longer the context, the bigger the savings.
Let's compare my architecture with dense attention, using the same number of layers in both.
At 1K, with ten layers, that's about 150 times fewer attention connections.
At 8K, with thirteen layers, it's already over a thousand times fewer.
And at 1M, with twenty layers? About 140,000 times fewer.
That's over 99.999 percent of the pairwise attention computation gone.

Now, say those attention interactions account for 40 to 60 percent of the model's total computation.
Then, on paper, I could cut roughly 40 to 60 percent of the total computation at 1M.
Not just the attention part. The whole model.
That's incredible!

---

But when I tried to turn that theory into code,
things started going wrong.

I started with plain PyTorch: gather the selected words, compute the scores, apply softmax, and combine the values.
Simple enough. But each little operation comes with its own overhead.
I had removed most of the math, not most of the time.

So I tried the built-in attention operator, SDPA.
Then I wrote fused Triton kernels, combining those operations so the GPU wouldn't have to keep starting another tiny job.

And I kept pushing the CUDA execution: process several positions together, keep intermediate results inside the kernel, and reorganize the backward pass to avoid competing gradient writes.
With grouped-query attention, I also stopped copying the same keys and values across multiple heads.

All that, just to make the GPU do less work by doing less work.
Apparently, that needed explaining.

Kernel launch overhead and data movement were eating into the savings.
The calculation was tiny. The cost of getting it started wasn't.

---

But wait. Would that overhead still matter as much with a longer context?

I moved the test to an RTX 4090 with 48 gigabytes of VRAM.
And yes, I checked the competition.
The standard causal attention was already using a fused FlashAttention kernel.
My version used fused Triton kernels.
Both ran the same eight-layer model, with batch size one.

At 4K, my version was actually a little slower.
At 16K, they were about even.
At 64K, mine was over five times faster.
And at one million positions?

Standard attention: about 64,000 tokens per second.
Mine: about 3.57 million.

That's nearly 56 times faster.
Okay. Now the GPU is finally noticing all that missing math.

But this was forward only: no backward pass, no optimizer, and no vocabulary output layer.
And the model had only eight layers, so my binary route couldn't connect the entire million-position window yet.
This test measured speed. It didn't prove that the model could use all that context.

---

So I tried actual training on WikiText-2.

First, a quick one-million-position test: three passes, with a tiny four-layer model.
Standard attention took about thirteen and a half minutes.
Mine took about twelve seconds, including kernel compilation.
Around 66 times faster overall.

Sounds ridiculous. But again, four layers don't cover a million positions.
So I needed a test where the route could actually reach across the whole window.

I used 65,536 positions and sixteen attention layers.
Same model width, same parameter count, same training data, same twenty passes.
I also kept WikiText's validation split separate.

Standard attention finished in about 26 minutes.
Mine finished in under five.
About 5.5 times faster, for the complete training run.

And the loss?
Training cross-entropy went from 2.32 with standard attention to 1.33 with mine.
On held-out validation text, it went from 2.30 to 1.37.
Lower is better.

So in this run, I got faster training and lower loss.
This is still a small character-level model and one random seed.
But it's a result worth following up on.

The memory numbers were almost identical: about 5.1 gigabytes each.
FlashAttention already avoids storing that giant square attention matrix.
And the rest of the model still needs memory in both versions.
Fewer connections saved computation here, without making the whole model dramatically smaller in memory.

---


So let me get this straight.
We spend thousands of dollars on a GPU,
then ask it to calculate a mountain of connections that a much smaller graph might replace.

At one million positions, my graph uses about 0.0007 percent of the attention connections.
Roughly one in 140,000.
That's the size of the cut on paper.
Put it another way: if I'm paying for 140,000 times as many connections,
I'd like to see those connections earn their keep.
Where's the spectacular improvement to match that spectacular amount of work?
Looking at the loss, I don't see it.
In the deeper training test, the sparse version even came out ahead.

So how much of today's AI industry's computing bill comes from historical baggage?
Architectures we keep using because the hardware is good at running them,
and hardware we keep building because those are the architectures everyone uses.

Imagine a GPU looking very busy,
while a huge chunk of its work is calculating dense connections we might not need.
Then I remove those calculations, and it spends more of its time waiting for data or the next kernel launch.
Great. I've given my expensive GPU less work, and somehow more time off.

So here are a few interesting questions.
Maybe some evil little details make this overhead unavoidable.
Or a different hardware architecture could just solve the problem.

And I might look into this in future videos,
including AI hardware design, but also anything else about AI.
And that's another big rabbit hole.
